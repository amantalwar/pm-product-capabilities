# Initiative Kickoff

## What this orchestrates

This is a sequence, not a single technique: it takes a raw idea through a fast one-pager, a real alternatives
check, an investment justification, stakeholder mapping, and a formal approval ask, ending with an initiative that
is funded, resourced, and ready to hand to delivery. Each stage exists to stop a bad or under-argued idea before it
reaches the expensive stage of asking for money and headcount. This is the right skill for "should this become a
real initiative and how do I get it approved" — not for refining an initiative that's already been greenlit, and
not for a single narrow deliverable like just the business case or just the stakeholder map on their own.

## Stages

1. **Fast one-pager** — `initiative-canvas`
   Capture the idea's problem, hypothesis, target outcome, and rough size on a single page before any deep work.
   This stage is deliberately shallow — its job is to decide whether the idea deserves the deeper stages that
   follow, not to fully argue the case yet.
   **Gate to proceed:** the one-pager's problem statement and target outcome are specific enough that a stranger
   reading it would know what success looks like — not just "improve X."

2. **Check real alternatives** — `solution-options-generator`
   Generate genuine alternative approaches (not just straw men) and compare them against the chosen direction.
   The point isn't to talk yourself out of the idea, it's to make sure the business case in the next stage is
   built against a direction that actually earned its place.
   **Gate to proceed:** at least 2-3 real alternatives were considered and the chosen approach beats them on stated
   criteria — not just described after the fact as "the obvious choice."

3. **Justify the investment** — `business-case`
   Build the cost/benefit case: what this costs, what it returns, and over what time horizon.
   **Gate to proceed:** the business case has a number an exec can react to (cost, expected return, payback period)
   — not just qualitative upside language.

4. **Identify who approves and who's informed** — `stakeholder-mapper`
   Map who needs to formally approve this, who needs to be consulted, and who just needs to be told. Do this
   before writing the ask, not after, so the ask in stage 5 is addressed to the right person from the start.
   **Gate to proceed:** there's a named approver (not "leadership" in the abstract) and a short list of people
   whose objections would actually block this if not addressed beforehand.

5. **Make the approval ask** — `decision-memo`
   Turn stages 1-4 into a single-page ask with a clear recommendation for the named approver.
   **Gate to proceed:** the approver has given an actual Yes/No/Modified answer — not "looks good, let's talk more"
   — before this initiative is considered kicked off.

## Sequencing notes

This kickoff sequence exists to make sure money and headcount only get requested once the idea has survived a real
check, not just an internal one.

- Stages 1-2 can partially overlap — generating alternatives often reshapes the one-pager — but stage 3 (business
  case) should not start until the alternatives check has actually picked a direction, or the cost/benefit case
  gets built for the wrong option. Revising the one-pager after stage 2 is normal and expected, not a sign
  something went wrong.
- Stage 4 (stakeholder mapping) can run in parallel with stage 3 (business case) since they draw on different
  information, but both must finish before stage 5 — the decision memo needs both the numbers and the named
  approver to be useful. Running stage 5 before either finishes just produces a memo that gets sent back for
  rework once the missing piece surfaces.
- **The most common skipped stage is #2 (real alternatives)** — teams have already mentally committed to their
  favored approach by the time they write the one-pager, so this stage becomes a rubber stamp instead of a real
  check. What breaks downstream: the business case (stage 3) gets built to justify a pre-decided answer, and if a
  skeptical approver in stage 5 asks "why not just do X instead," there's no real answer because X was never
  seriously evaluated — the approval stalls and the whole sequence has to loop back to stage 2 anyway.
- A second trap: treating stage 4 (stakeholder mapping) as an afterthought done right before sending the memo.
  Mapped late, it tends to surface only the people already in the room, missing the quieter stakeholder whose
  objection derails approval after the ask has already gone out.

## Final output

An approved, resourced initiative package: the one-pager (stage 1), the alternatives comparison showing why this
approach won (stage 2), the business case with cost/benefit numbers (stage 3), the stakeholder map naming approver
and key influencers (stage 4), and the decision memo with a recorded Yes/No/Modified answer (stage 5) — handed to
delivery as the agreed mandate, budget, and scope to start work against. Delivery should be able to open this
package and know what they're building toward, what they're allowed to spend, and who to go back to if the scope
needs to change mid-flight.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Does the business case's numbers change if you plug in the runner-up alternative instead of the chosen one —
      i.e., was the comparison in stage 2 real?
- [ ] Is there a named individual who said yes, not just a general sense that "the team is aligned"?
- [ ] Would someone on the delivery team receiving this package know the budget, the target outcome, and who to
      escalate to without asking follow-up questions?
- [ ] Did at least one stakeholder outside the initiative's originating team get a chance to raise an objection
      before approval, not after?
- [ ] If the approver came back with "Modified," was the modification actually folded back into the one-pager and
      business case, rather than treated as a side conversation?
