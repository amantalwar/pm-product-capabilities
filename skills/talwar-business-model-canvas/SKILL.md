# Business Model Canvas

## Why this matters

The value of the canvas isn't the 9 boxes — it's that they're connected, and drawing them side by side forces you to
test consistency across a business model instead of describing each piece in isolation. A value proposition that
doesn't map to a specific, named customer segment is a slogan. A channel that assumes high-touch enterprise sales
next to a customer relationship block that says "self-serve" is a contradiction the canvas exists to catch before
you build around it. Treat every block as a hypothesis about one connected system, and treat the canvas as done only
when you've traced at least one customer segment across all nine and found no contradiction — that's the exercise,
not filling in the grid.

## Gather inputs first

Before drafting, get (ask if not given, and make a reasonable assumption out loud if told to proceed):

- **Target customer segment(s)** — as specific as possible, not "everyone" or "SMBs"
- **Current or hypothesized value proposition**
- **Whether this is a new venture** (all 9 blocks are hypotheses) **or an existing business being re-examined**
  (some blocks are known facts, others are the ones actually in question)
- **Any known cost or revenue data** to ground the cost structure and revenue streams blocks

## Process

1. **Customer segments** — name who, specifically. If there's more than one segment, keep them distinct through
   every later block rather than blending them.
2. **Value propositions** — the job done for each segment. Map each value proposition to the segment(s) it serves;
   a value prop with no segment attached is a hypothesis not yet tested.
3. **Channels** — how the value proposition actually reaches each segment (awareness, purchase, delivery).
4. **Customer relationships** — self-serve, high-touch, community-led, automated — and check this is consistent
   with the channel choice, not contradicting it.
5. **Revenue streams** — how each segment pays and for what mechanism (subscription, usage, transaction fee, one-
   time), not just the word "subscription" with no further detail.
6. **Key resources, key activities, key partnerships** — what's required to actually deliver the value proposition;
   these should be traceable back to specific value props, not a generic capabilities list.
7. **Cost structure** — driven by the resources/activities/partnerships above, not invented independently.
8. **Stress-test**: pick one customer segment and trace it through all 9 blocks. Look for a contradiction (e.g., a
   self-serve relationship paired with a channel that only reaches enterprise buyers) and resolve it before calling
   the canvas final.

## Output template

```markdown
# Business Model Canvas: [Product/Business name]

## Customer segments
[Specific, named segments]

## Value propositions
[Mapped 1:1 or clearly linked to the segments above]

## Channels
[How value reaches each segment]

## Customer relationships
[Self-serve / high-touch / community / automated — per segment, consistent with channels]

## Revenue streams
[Pricing mechanism per segment — not just "subscription," specify unit and price logic]

## Key resources
[What's required to deliver the value propositions]

## Key activities
[What the business must do well, tied to the value propositions]

## Key partnerships
[Who's relied on externally, and for what]

## Cost structure
[Costs driven by the resources/activities/partnerships above]

## Consistency check
[The one customer segment traced across all 9 blocks, and the contradiction found and resolved — or "none found"]
```

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Every value proposition names the specific segment it serves
- [ ] Revenue streams specify a pricing mechanism, not just a business-model buzzword
- [ ] Cost structure items are traceable to specific resources, activities, or partnerships listed above
- [ ] At least one customer segment was traced across all 9 blocks and checked for internal contradiction
